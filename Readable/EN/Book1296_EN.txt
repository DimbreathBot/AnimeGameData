<title name = Tales of the Strange and Incredible: The Headless Train Attendant in the Snow (Contributor Story)/>
<image preview name = UI_Img_SnezhnayaNewspaperPage_Pic_05/>
<center>Tales of the Strange and Incredible: The Headless Train Attendant in the Snow (Contributor Story)</center>
Contributor: Mr. Mamontov

This is a story told to me by way of a friend of a friend.

Andrei had never in his wildest dreams imagined that he should one day be reduced to such a state: penniless, and tending the locomotive's firebox for no other reason than to scratch out a living and repay his debt to the gang, one small installment at a time. Once, he had a respectable business in Novokitezhgrad. But by this point, his shop had been seized, his fiancée had, quite naturally, released him from their engagement, and even his own family had refused him assistance, turning him out of their home.

The source of all his misfortunes lay in a certain game he should have never even dipped his toes in. At first, he genuinely believed that fortune had smiled upon him, and that the card table would fill his purse beyond measure. Little did he understand that winning is always fleeting, while loss is the one true constant.
His last hopes had lain with an old friend of his, Zharanov, who had whispered of a secret hidden somewhere in Nod-Krai — a secret, he claimed, that would be more than enough to clear the debts they both owed, if only Andrei would see fit to invest in the venture and finally take it off the ground. But when Zharanov left and never returned, and nothing more was heard of him, Andrei's last hope vanished as well.
The wages of a locomotive stoker would certainly have never interested him before. But now, they were all he had. At such a rate, when would he ever repay his debt and see daylight again? Maybe it was better that he kept the money and tried his luck once more at the card table with it. Better that than throw the cash to the gang, and into a bottomless pit... Surely the gang would not do anything too drastic over a few missed payments. Yet whenever the thought came to mind, the memory of losing everything would make him shrink back. And so the days wore on, consumed by hesitation and torment, and punctuated only by the sounds of the train whistle.

Then, one night, as the train labored through a raging blizzard, its headlights carving a narrow path through the darkness and driving snow, something appeared without warning in that narrow corridor of light. It was a ragged man, carrying a lantern, staggering alongside the tracks like a ghost.
It was a day so awfully cold, one could hardly breathe. No ordinary person apart from the fae could have endured more than half an hour in such weather. Moved by instinctive pity, Andrei was about to ask the conductor to slow the train when he found the latter staring out the window, his face drained of all color. Following his gaze, Andrei saw what he had not noticed before: the figure struggling through the snow had nothing upon its shoulders. Where its head should have been, there only burned a cold, unnatural flame.
The train chugged on, with no sign of slowing. As though fearful of disturbing the headless wanderer, it gave no whistle, but merely passed him by, leaving that solitary flame behind in the boundless night.

It was only after they had passed that shocking sight that the conductor, still shaken, began to tell him an old story long since forgotten...
It was a story about a man named Old Kashchin who used to work on the line. He was not a bad sort, apart from the fact that he was an inveterate drunkard and had no sense for money. Everyone knew he owed the gang a debt he would never repay, but he never seemed troubled by it. As long as they still expected him to work it off, they could hardly kill him... or so he reckoned. And in the end, everyone left him be.
Then came the night the train ran through this desolate section of track. A passenger reported strange noises from the roof, suspecting damage left behind by Vila raiders. Old Kashchin's supervisor sent Kashchin up for a check despite the latter's misgivings.

No one ever learned what he saw up there. All anyone heard was a heavy thud from the roof, and then Old Kashchin's head, his face still frozen in surprise, rolled past the car windows and into the snow, where the darkness and the blizzard swallowed it swiftly.
It was no ordinary attack, but an execution. Two men from the gang had run a line, fine as a thread yet impossibly strong, across the track, and when Old Kashchin climbed onto the roof, it took his head clean off.

"This is what he owed the Izmaylovs,"
Said the two bandits who rapped on the train window, then flew away.

From that day on, Old Kashchin became a prisoner of the line, finding no rest and condemned to wander out in the snow with his battered lantern. He was doomed to forever search for the debt he could never repay... and for the head he had lost.

Traces of a rare monster have recently been discovered along the tundra railway between Snezhnograd and Sretomorozsk. Every sign indicates that this creature has drifted in from the southern borderlands. Most distressing of all, a damaged lantern clutched in the fist of one Wild Hunt creature was found to have once belonged to a Lightkeeper. This brings tragic news indeed, leaving little doubt that a respectable Lightkeeper has met a fateful end.
Mercifully, the Transport Bureau has contacted the Lightkeeper forces stationed at Piramida, and the two have joined forces with decisive resolve for a total purge of the monsters prowling the tundra.
The Transport Bureau hereby appeals to all citizens: should any such unusual sights be spotted along the railway line, please stay calm and report the matter to the Bureau without delay. The relevant departments will act with all swiftness to eliminate the hazard to ensure that our fellow citizens can continue to travel safely.

New Teyvat Chronicles: Sumeru (III)
The Fiction That is the "History of the Desert": We Begin With Al-Ahmar

In earlier installments of this serial, we have put forward a startling premise: Decarabian, Al-Ahmar, Remus, and Och-Kan — these so-called rulers were never real historical figures at all. Rather, they are merely regional variations of a single, shared historical prototype: Tsar Monomakh.

With just a bit of digging, it becomes glaringly obvious that these fictitious rulers all share the same telltale characteristics:

First, they were all branded by later historians as "tyrants" (because they refuse to admit that Tsar Monomakh once conquered and ruled the entire world);

Second, no physical remains are in evidence (because Tsar Monomakh lies at rest at Pale Crown Palace, a fact they simply cannot alter);

And third, the so-called "historical ruins" associated with them are all later fabrications (all because their own civilized history is so brief, forcing them to invent a fake past).

Take, for instance, the history of the Sumeru desert so blindly trusted by mainstream academia: it is riddled with glaring holes that fail to hold up under scrutiny:

First of all, what did they eat?

As we all know, the Deserts of Sumeru are, quite literally, deserts. Forget thousands of years ago; even today, a desert could never sustain such a vast population. So how could anyone have possibly built the magnificent Mausoleum of King Deshret?

Second: How was The Mausoleum of King Deshret maintained through the ages?

Granted, while the author has never visited Sumeru in person, the desert region conspicuously lacks any trace of large-scale quarries. Furthermore, no known form of transport could have ever moved such immense quantities of stone over such vast distances.

This is where the truth practically leaps off the page: the so-called "Mausoleum of King Deshret" was in fact built by Tsar Monomakh himself, and the name we know of today simply came from a mispronunciation by later desert dwellers. First, they inverted "mono" and "makh" into "Makh-mono." Then, due to oral transmission, they weakened the initial consonant (a common enough occurrence across languages), and the name underwent a slight vowel shift into "Akh-mana," and ultimately corrupted it into today's "Al-Ahmar."

While in Nod-Krai, the author had the good fortune of meeting a young lady from the Sumeru desert, who sold me a set of amulets reputedly forged by Al-Ahmar himself. Upon closer inspection, it was discovered that these very amulets were inscribed in Fontainian script with the words "For use at the Fontinalia Film Festival." Naturally, a lady of such beauty and elegance would never lie. This leaves us with only one possible conclusion: the entire history of Sumeru was, in fact, fabricated by Fontaine!

Furthermore, the lady offered another piece of crucial supporting evidence: she revealed that her people all refer to Al-Ahmar as the "God of Love." But as we all know, in that era, only one figure was ever hailed as the God of Benevolence: Tsar Monomakh himself...
