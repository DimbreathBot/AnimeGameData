Freight train I-330 recently derailed while traveling from Snezhnograd to Novokitezhgrad. Despite the accident, no casualties were reported.

According to initial reports, the conductor on duty — identified here by the pseudonym Mr. Turkin — resorted to a series of unusually aggressive emergency measures. His actions ultimately enabled all passengers and crew to escape unharmed. What was initially hailed, and widely followed, as an "emergency rescue," however, has since come under closer scrutiny. With preliminary investigative procedures now underway, specific aspects of the operation are being formally reviewed, and questions have been raised over accountability for some of the measures taken.

The preliminary accident report focuses on a split-second decision that ran counter to standard safety protocol. Mr. Turkin claimed that, on the day of the incident, he saw a landslide suddenly develop directly in the train's path. Believing that conventional emergency braking could not bring the train to a halt within the remaining distance, he instead chose to break up the consist. The fully loaded freight cars at the rear were forcibly uncoupled and left behind. With only the locomotive and personnel carriages remaining, Mr. Turkin accelerated through the landslide zone.

The personnel carriages ultimately cleared the affected area, with only minor abrasions reported among some passengers. The abandoned freight cars, however, were completely buried, along with the substantial volume of state supplies they had been carrying. The full extent of the resulting property loss remains difficult to determine.

The Railway Transport Bureau has since opened a formal post-incident review and accountability process. At the center of the inquiry is evidence that may call Mr. Turkin's account into question. A technical specialist involved in the preliminary assessment, speaking to this newspaper on condition of anonymity, said the line's warning systems had issued the hazard alert in advance and in accordance with established procedure. "Under the standard emergency response plan, the conductor should have applied the emergency brakes the moment the alert was received," the specialist said. "Uncoupling and abandoning the freight cars without authorization was a serious violation, as was accelerating through the disaster zone with the personnel carriages in tow. Both actions breached the Standard Operating Procedures for Rail Transport Safety."

The accident commission's final report must now determine whether Mr. Turkin's actions constituted a justified response to an extreme emergency — or a serious overreaction arising from a flawed assessment of the danger. It must also establish whether the valuable supplies left behind were truly beyond saving. Until those findings are released, the line between heroism and misconduct remains unresolved. This newspaper will continue to follow the investigation.

"Indeed, you are entirely correct," Grand Duke Brahe Trubetskoy replied with serene composure. "Your poor wife has always been thus — forever yearning after a life of unbounded luxury, and resolved to live, and spend, with all the prodigality of a true grand duchess."

"So... am I to understand, Your Excellency, that my wife stole your platinum chess piece?" The color fled Viscount Shuisky's face, leaving it as white as the snow beyond the windows. Almost at once, however, his complexion darkened to a livid grey. The inference was so preposterous, and the shock of it so profound, that he appeared scarcely able to comprehend what he had heard. "No, no... How could she possibly have done such a thing?"

"Vanity is her great weakness," the Grand Duke continued at his leisure. "But you, Andrei Dmitriyevich, possess only the rank and revenues of a viscount. You might have ground every unfortunate soul upon your estates beneath the millstone, pressing them like Rimecurrants until not a drop of oil remained to be wrung from them, and still her insatiable vanity would have demanded more. And thus resentment began to put forth its first shoots within your heart, rank as brambles and thornweed. That, however, was only part of the matter. Your noble and kindly heart had found another object for its affections: Miss Milady de Este, the celebrated Fontainian diva lately arrived from the Opera Epiclese. Is it not so? Oh, pray do not misunderstand me. I have no wish to reproach you for loving her. Indeed, what man could behold so pampered and imperious a beauty — so dazzling in her ostentation, like a peacock in full display — and not feel his heart stirred? No, it was not your love that condemned you, but what you did in its name. For her sake, you murdered your wife. You appropriated the treasure I had entrusted to your keeping, a precious relic bestowed upon me by His late Imperial Majesty. And, most contemptible of all, you fastened the sordid charge of absconding with the valuables upon your poor dead wife, knowing that she could no longer utter a word in her own defense. Ah, even one dog would not treat another so! And your wife was so beautiful... and loved you so dearly."

The Viscount kept his eyes fixed upon the Grand Duke's almost preternaturally handsome face. Meanwhile, as though guided by an impulse of which he was scarcely conscious, his right hand slipped deep into the pocket of his overcoat. His cracked lips quivered. He seemed on the verge of speaking, yet no sound emerged. He merely opened and closed his mouth like a fish cast upon the shore. At length, he lurched to his feet. A look of sorrow, almost of self-pity, had settled over his features, and when at last he found his voice, it came harsh and hoarse.

"My good man, my dear Brahe Prokhorovitch, what extraordinary notion is this? I beg you, hear me before you proceed any further... From whose contemptible lips did you receive so baseless a calumny, conceived in malice and fashioned wholly from lies? And why should you — a man whom I have always esteemed as good — choose to inflict such torment upon a bereaved husband, accusing him, His Majesty preserve us, of having murdered the wife dearest to his heart? You must pardon the liberty, Brahe Prokhorovitch, but are you fully master of your reason? It is beyond comprehension, beyond all comprehension... You would impute so monstrous a deed to me? To me, Andrei Dmitriyevich Shuisky, a gentleman upon whom His Majesty himself conferred the dignity of a title? How deeply you have wounded me! Yet the respect due to your exalted station compels me to say it once more: I have done nothing wicked, nothing contrary to heaven or conscience. As for this accusation that I appropriated the platinum chess piece, it is an injustice beyond measure, sheer nonsense from beginning to end. Perhaps you have drunk rather too much hot tea. Perhaps some delirium has seized you... But no matter. Have it as you will! Send men to search my residence this very instant. Let them prise up every floorboard and strip the plaster from every wall. If you find so much as—"

"Then be so good as to remove your glove. The one upon your right hand, if memory serves."
